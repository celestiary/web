## 1. The Parker-like approach, and how well the model reproduces Parker

reconstructed from Parker's published perihelion times and distances and oriented to put the
craft on Venus's bearing at each flyby. Nothing forces the legs to meet at Venus, so the miss

## 2. The sun-synchronous relay orbit

The photosphere at the equator turns once in 24.47 days (sidereal; Snodgrass & Ulrich 1990).
A circular orbit with that period sits at **35.47 Rs (0.165 AU)**,
moving at 73.3 km/s,
in 50 kW/m² of sunlight (37 suns).

That is almost exactly Parker's *first* perihelion, 35.66 Rs. Parker's final orbit dips far
inside the synchronous radius; what the relay needs is not a lower perihelion but a lower
aphelion, and Venus cannot provide that, because every Venus-flyby orbit still reaches Venus.

Simulated plan, from the final 88-day orbit:

| Burn | Date | Δv (km/s) |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Raise perihelion to synchronous radius (at aphelion) | 2027-01-15 | 9.17 |
| Circularize at synchronous radius | 2027-03-11 | 20.35 |
| Lander deorbit to the photosphere | 2027-04-29 | 56.16 |

Relay total: **29.5 km/s**. For comparison Parker carried well under 1 km/s of propellant.

Alternatives the model sizes but does not animate:

| Option | Δv (km/s) | Note |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Stop after Venus flyby 1 and circularize at perihelion 1 | 22.2 | One burn; skips flybys 2–7 |
| Match the rotation period but stay eccentric | 8.3 | One burn at perihelion; orbit 9.86 × 61.1 Rs, over the same longitude once per rotation, not continuously |

The two temperatures are radiative equilibrium with nothing done about it: a black plate facing the
Sun with an insulated back, and a grey sphere radiating from its whole surface, which is the
lander's shape.

**Passive hull limit.** The concept's hull material melts near 4,100 °C (4,373 K). The sphere
reaches that by sunlight alone 0.06 Rs (44,349 km) above the photosphere,
10.0 min before arrival, and would settle at 4,854 K at the photosphere itself.
That is about 480 K over the limit, a narrower gap than the 1,400 °C the concept doc
works from, because half the sphere's sky is still cold space until it sinks into the gas.
Everything below that height has to be paid for by ablation, active cooling or reflectivity; this
counts sunlight only, not the heating from moving through the gas at 600 km/s.

A minimum-energy descent arrives level, not falling: it spends its last
2.1 min below 2,000 km, skimming about
76,000 km round the Sun as it sinks.
A straight drop crosses the same 2,000 km in about 3 seconds.

## 4. What the numbers say

- **Every route arrives at about 610 km/s.** That is the Sun's escape speed
  less a little; it is set by
  the depth of the gravity well, not by the route. Shedding it is the landing problem.
- **The relay orbit is expensive and the lander delivery is cheap, from different orbits.** The relay
  costs 22.2–29.5 km/s; a lander dropped from aphelion costs
  8.1 km/s. Dropping the
  lander *from* the relay orbit is the worst case at 56.2 km/s.
- **A minimum-energy descent lands out of the relay's sight**, half an orbit round while the relay
  has moved only 66.3°. The steeper the descent, the nearer it lands to the relay.
- **The sling wheel cannot shed that speed.** An untapered spoke fails at a rim speed of √(2σ/ρ): 2.8 km/s for
  carbon fibre (7 GPa, 1790 kg/m³; values from memory, check them), which is
  0.46% of the
  arrival speed. Tapering buys a small multiple of that at exponential mass cost, not a factor of 200.
- **Light pressure is the one force that scales with gravity here.** Both fall as 1/r², so a reflector lighter than
  1.53 g/m² (all-up mass over sail area) is pushed out harder than it is pulled in, at any distance, and
  could descend slowly instead of falling. Not modelled; it trades the speed problem for a materials one.
